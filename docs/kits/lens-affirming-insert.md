This insert doesn't ask anyone to come out, be outed, or explain themselves. It doesn't change the kit it came with. It adds a few prompts, three sentences a host can say, and a delivery mode that keeps the outside of the envelope quiet. If nobody at your table needs it, you won't notice it's there. If somebody does, they'll notice nobody made a thing of it.

The box also put the whole order in discreet mode. Page 4 explains that.

- Time: five minutes to read. The prompts take the time the host kit's activity already takes.

## Page 1. What this insert is, and the house rule

You bought a holiday kit and checked a box. The box added four pages. Not a new activity, not a lesson, not a talk. The kit works the way it worked before; this leans on it a little, so when the family sits down, everybody who's coming is already welcome and nobody had to announce it.

**The house rule:** anyone can pass. On any prompt, in any kit, on any night. Nobody explains why. The host doesn't circle back. Say it out loud once at the start and the whole insert is already working.

**Three hosting notes.**
- Say people's names the way they gave them to you. If somebody arrives with a name or a person you haven't met, say both like you've always said them.
- Don't introduce anybody as anything. "This is Devin" is complete.
- Somebody isn't at the table this year, and it may be because of who they love. You don't have to name them. Set the table like they might walk in.

## Page 2. The prompts, matched to your kit

Eight prompts; your kit uses three or four, and the table says which. Anybody can answer any of them without saying one thing about themselves they didn't plan to. That was the test.

1. "Say your name, and who you came with if you want to. That's it."
2. "Who taught you how this family does the holidays? Who are you teaching?"
3. "What's a rule in this house you're glad somebody changed?"
4. "Who's not here who should be? Say a name, or just hold the thought."
5. "What's something you cook, sing, or say that came from a friend and not a relative?"
6. "What's one thing this family got right this year about making room?"
7. "What's the weather at your seat right now? One word. No reasons."
8. "What do you want the youngest person here to remember about how we treated each other tonight?"

|---|---|---|
| Winter Light Make | 1, 2, 5, 6 | One per craft night, before hands get busy |
| Winter Light Practice | 1, 3, 7, 8 | Alongside the kit's own check-ins |

**What the prompts don't do.** None of them asks who you love, what you are, or what happened. Prompt 5 is the closest, and it's about a friend's cornbread. If a prompt makes anyone at your table feel looked at, cut it. Three still works.

## Page 3. Three things a host can say

The host scripts from Open House, word for word, because any kit can hit the same moment: somebody says something. One person you trust should know them before the day.

**Script one, the redirect (first offense, low heat):**
> "Not tonight. Pass the yams."

Said warmly, said once, then the conversation moves. Most of the time that's enough. It doesn't argue; it declines.

**Script two, the boundary (it continues, or it was aimed at someone):**
> "In this house everybody who's here is welcome. That includes you. Let's leave it there."

Said to the relative, not about them. "That includes you" is doing the work: it keeps them at the table while ending the subject.

**Script three, the exit (it's not stopping):**
> "I'm going to step out with [name] for a minute. Y'all keep eating."

The host or trusted person leaves the room with the person who was targeted, or with the relative, whichever is right. Not a scene. The table keeps eating. The evening is not ruined; one conversation is over.

**What the scripts don't do:** they don't explain, educate, or convince. That's not the host's job at their own table on a holiday. Winning the argument isn't the goal. Everybody still being at the table is.
