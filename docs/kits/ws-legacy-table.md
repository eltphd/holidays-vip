# Legacy Table — draft 1 (for audit round 1)

**Category:** Wellness Season · **Domain:** togetherness / intergenerational · **Format:** make + practice · **Kit id:** `ws-legacy-table` · **Price:** $34 digital · **Files by:** Dec 12 (Kwanzaa / New Year week) · **File name:** `ws-legacy-table-digital.pdf`
**Who it's for:** a family with an elder in it, and the elder. Also the teen who's going to hold the phone.
**One line:** What we're carrying forward.

**Owners for audit:** older adult (all of it; the interview and the letter especially). The adolescent reads it for "does this ask me to disclose anything."

---

## Before you open this (read this first)

This kit is for the week the whole family is already in the room, and for the person at the table who's been to the most of these. It keeps the stories on that person's terms, and gives the tree room for everybody who's actually family.

These are practices, not treatment. Each one comes from somewhere, and we say where. We don't say what any of it will do for you or for the elder. Asking somebody about the first house they remember is a good afternoon. That's the whole claim.

The house rule, for every page: **anyone can pass.** Any question, any paragraph, any name, any night; the elder most of all. Nobody has to answer, be recorded, be on the tree, or be in the photo.

> **This isn't therapy.** These are practices, not treatment. We name where each one comes from; we don't promise what it will do. If someone at your table needs more than a practice, reach for a professional. If it's heavier than a practice can hold, these are people who pick up: **988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline** — call or text 988, any hour. **Call BlackLine** — 1-800-604-5841, peer support by us, for us, with an LGBTQ+ Black femme lens. **Trans Lifeline** — 877-565-8860, run by trans people, for trans people, weekdays 1–9 PM Eastern.

**What this asks of you**
- Time: the interview is 30 to 45 minutes, or one question a day across the week. The tree is an evening. The letter is an hour alone, or a week of ten minutes. Kuumba night sits inside a night you were already having.
- Sensory: a phone on the table, not in anyone's face; paper and pens; candles or battery lights on Kuumba night; one loud moment (the toast); some crying, which is allowed; no required touch.
- Low-demand version: at the end. Three things. Every section has a stopping point, because the elder gets tired and the elder decides when it's over.

## 1. The oral-history interview (phone-recorded; the elder runs the recording)

Where it comes from: the way stories got kept before anybody wrote them down. Somebody asked, somebody told, somebody younger remembered. The phone is just the somebody younger.

**The rules, before the first question.** The person being interviewed controls the recording. Ask out loud: "Is it all right if I record this?" If no, take notes or nothing, and ask anyway. If yes, the phone sits face up in front of the elder, or in the elder's hand. The elder presses stop. "Stop that" stops it; "take that part out" takes it out, no discussion; "next" moves to the next question. When it's done, the recording belongs to the elder, and the family gets a copy if the elder says so.

**Who asks.** The youngest person who can hold still and stay quiet. The whole job is to ask and then not fill the silence; elders take their time getting to the good part. No follow-ups that begin with "why." "Tell me more," or nothing. And don't correct dates: if it was 1972 and the elder says 1974, it was 1974 this afternoon.

Time: 30 to 45 minutes, or one question a day for the week, which is often better. Sensory: a quiet room, the elder's own chair, the phone on the table, no camera unless the elder asks for one. Low-demand: the four starred questions, or one of them.

**The questions.** Ask them in any order. Skip any.

1. What's the first house you remember, and who was in it? ★
2. Who named you, and did they ever tell you why?
3. What did the kitchen smell like on a holiday when you were small? Whoever's kitchen it was.
4. Tell me about somebody who's gone that you want us to know about. ★
5. What did you have to figure out on your own that nobody taught you?
6. What's something you did that you were proud of, that nobody made a fuss over?
7. What did the holidays look like when you were my age?
8. Who was family to you that wasn't blood? ★
9. What's something you changed your mind about?
10. What do you want kept, and what do you want let go?
11. Is there anything you want to say to somebody who'll hear this later? ★

**After.** The elder decides what happens to the file: keep it, send it to one person, send it to everybody, or delete it. Delete is a real answer. If it's kept, name it with a first name and the year, nothing else.

## 2. The family tree that includes chosen family

Where it comes from: every Black family that was ever raised by a play-cousin, a church mother, a neighbor who kept the kids, or the friend who became an uncle without anybody voting on it. Half the people who raised most of us wouldn't fit on the chart they hand you at the library. This is the tree they fit on.

**The one rule for getting on the tree.** Somebody at the table says you belong. That's the whole rule. No boxes for how: no "step," no "half," no "adopted," no "ex," no "biological." A line between two names means "these two are family to each other," and that's all a line means. Anyone who wants to say what a person was to them can write it on the back, in their own words, and doesn't have to.

**Nobody has to say who their person is.** A name said without a reason goes on. If your own name is on the tree and you'd rather it weren't, say so and it comes off, no reason needed.

Time: about 45 minutes at the table, or across the week as names come up. Sensory: one big sheet of paper, pens, people talking over each other; somebody will cry. Low-demand: one name each, no lines. A page of names is a tree.

```
                     THIS TABLE, ___________ (year)

  Names go on in the order they're said out loud. No order is the right order.
  A line between two names means: these two are family to each other.
  A name can have as many lines as it has people.

     _______________       _______________       _______________

     _______________       _______________       _______________

     _______________       _______________       _______________

     _______________       _______________       _______________

  People who aren't here, said by somebody who is:

     _______________  (said by _____________)
     _______________  (said by _____________)
     _______________  (said by _____________)

  Back of the page: anything anybody wants to say about a name,
  in their own words. Optional. Nobody reads it out unless the writer does.
```

**How it goes.** Somebody starts with their own name, and names get written as they come. The elder puts on names nobody else remembers; the teen puts on names nobody else knows. The people who've passed go in the "said by" section, because they're still somebody's.

**What this tree isn't.** A genealogy chart: no birth dates, no "married to," no arrows for who came from whom. That's a project for another day. This one answers a different question: who's ours.

## 3. Recipe and photo archive prompts

Where it comes from: the recipe card in the tin and the shoebox of pictures, the two archives every family already has.

**The recipe card.** One card per dish, filled by whoever makes it now. Five lines:
- Who taught you this.
- What they left out when they told you.
- What you changed.
- What it's for (a day, a person, a mood).
- The part that can't be written down.

Time: fifteen minutes per dish, best done while it's cooking. Sensory: kitchen, or the table after. Low-demand: one dish, one line. "Who taught you this" is the line.

**The photo prompts.** Five photos from a phone or a shoebox, passed around. For each one, anybody who knows answers:
- Who's in it.
- Who took it.
- What happened right after.

Then one more: the picture that doesn't exist. Somebody in this family was never photographed well. Say the name, write it on a card, and put the card in with the photos.

A photo goes in the family's archive if the person in it would be all right with that. If you don't know, ask, or leave it out. That includes the people at the table tonight.

Time: thirty minutes. Sensory: passing a phone around, some laughing and some crying. Low-demand: one photo, one name.

## 4. The legacy letter (a pass on every paragraph)

Where it comes from: the letter in the Bible, the note in the recipe tin, the thing an elder says once at the end of a phone call that everybody remembers for thirty years. This puts it on a page while there's time.

Written by the elder, or by anybody who wants to leave one. To one person, the whole table, or somebody who isn't born yet. It isn't read out unless the writer says. A one-paragraph letter is a letter.

Time: an hour alone, or ten minutes a day for the week. Sensory: quiet, a pen, or a phone if writing is hard now; a voice note is a letter too. Low-demand: paragraph four, only.

```
To: _________________________
   (a person, "the table," "whoever's reading this in twenty years")

Paragraph 1 — Where I came from, the short version.               [ ] pass

Paragraph 2 — A thing I got right.                                 [ ] pass

Paragraph 3 — A thing I'd do differently, if you want it.          [ ] pass

Paragraph 4 — What I want you to keep.                             [ ] pass

Paragraph 5 — What I want you to let go, on my say-so.             [ ] pass

Close — however you sign things.

What happens to this letter:
   [ ] read it out    [ ] hand it over    [ ] seal it, date on the front    [ ] keep it
```

**On paragraph five.** Some things get carried for generations because nobody with the standing to say "put that down" ever said it. An elder has the standing. This paragraph is where they use it, if they want to. (Paragraph three isn't an apology unless the writer wants it to be.)

## 5. Kuumba night: "what we're carrying forward"

Kuumba is the sixth night of Kwanzaa, December 31. Creativity: to do always as much as we can, in the way we can, in order to leave our community more beautiful and beneficial than we inherited it. In most houses it's also the night of the Karamu, the feast. This kit doesn't replace what your family does on Karamu night; it sits inside it, after the food. Or on the first, if the thirty-first is spoken for. Or any night that week, if the family doesn't keep Kwanzaa.

Time: sixty to ninety minutes, inside an evening you were already having. Sensory: candles or battery lights, food, one loud moment (the toast), people coming and going, no required touch. Low-demand: the question once around, passes allowed; that's the night.

**The run of the night.**

1. **The light.** The sixth candle, all six, a battery one, or none. Whatever your house does.
2. **The table.** Put what the week made on it: the tree, the recipe cards, the photos, and the phone if the elder said the recording could be played (the elder presses play and stop; two minutes is plenty). Nothing goes on the table that its owner didn't put there.
3. **The question, once around.** "What are we carrying forward?" One thing each: a recipe, a name, a rule, a way of doing Christmas morning, a phrase the elder says. Somebody writes them on one page in the order said. Anyone can pass. The elder goes first or last, their choice.
4. **The one thing we make.** Kuumba is creativity, so the night makes one thing that didn't exist before it. The "carrying forward" page counts. So does the tree, a dish the teen designs for next year, or a playlist of songs from the interview. One is enough.
5. **The toast.** "To what we came from, and what we're carrying." Glass up. That's all.
6. **The end.** When the elder says, or when the food's gone.

## 6. The readout for the family (what it is, what it isn't)

**What it is.** One page the family keeps, filled at the end of the week from what's already there: the "carrying forward" page, the names on the tree, the recipe cards with a name on them. It's the same idea as the Soul Compass check-in in the other kits: a practice the family does for itself, and keeps. Three prompts, and anyone can pass:

- What we made this week that didn't exist before it (the tree, the recording, a letter, the list).
- The names we said out loud.
- One thing we're doing again next year.

Time: fifteen minutes. Sensory: paper, a pen, one person reading it back. Low-demand: one line, any of the three.

**What it isn't.** Not a survey, not a score, not a measurement of anybody, not sent to us. Nothing individual leaves the house: not the recording, the letter, the names on the tree, or what anyone said on their turn. The page stays in the house with the rest of what the week made.

After the season, Measurement Ally invites households to share a short aggregate season readout. That's separate from this page, only with the family's consent, only what the family chooses, and saying no changes nothing.

## The bare minimum (low-demand version)

One evening, not much left: do these three and nothing else.

1. Ask the elder one starred question, phone on the table if they said yes. Let the silence sit.
2. Put one name each on the tree. No lines.
3. On the thirty-first, or whatever night, say "what are we carrying forward" once around. Passes count.

That's a Legacy Table. Everything else in this kit is extra.

## Open questions for the auditors (round 1)

- Older adult: the eleven questions. Would you answer them at your own table, or are they museum questions? Which would you cut? What's missing? And mark any line in the kit that sounds like a social worker or a librarian wrote it.
- Older adult: "the elder presses stop." Control, or one more thing to manage? Would you rather name someone to run the phone?
- Older adult: the tree with no labels. Freedom, or erasure? Would you want an optional "what they were to me" line on the front?
- Older adult: Kuumba night on the thirty-first. Too much to add to Karamu? Move it to Imani, or off the Kwanzaa calendar?
- Adolescent: the phone-holding job. Do you want it, or does it feel like being volunteered?
