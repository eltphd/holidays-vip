## Before you open this (read this first)

These are practices, not treatment. Every one of them is something Black families already do. We name where each one comes from, and we don't promise what it will do. A hum is a hum. A walk is a walk. If it doesn't do anything, you lost ten minutes and you were with your people.

The rule in this house is the same as every kit we make: **anyone can pass.** Nobody has to do a practice. Nobody has to say why they're not doing it. Passing is a full answer.

> **This isn't therapy.** These are practices, not treatment. We name where each one comes from; we don't promise what it will do. If someone at your table needs more than a practice, reach for a professional. If it's heavier than a practice can hold, these are people who pick up: **988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline** — call or text 988, any hour. **Call BlackLine** — 1-800-604-5841, peer support by us, for us, with an LGBTQ+ Black femme lens. **Trans Lifeline** — 877-565-8860, run by trans people, for trans people, weekdays 1–9 PM Eastern.

Put that card where people can see it without asking. The fridge, or the inside of a cabinet door. It's its own page in the PDF so you can print it once and never mention it.

**What this asks of you**
- Time: each practice is two to thirty minutes. You don't do all of them. Most houses will do two across the whole season.
- Sensory: one practice is loud (the line dance). One is cold (the walk). The rest are quiet. Nothing requires touch, eye contact, or talking.
- Low-demand version: "The bare minimum" at the end. It's a real version. Nobody in the house has to know you're using it.

## 1. Practices we do together (with the root named)

Six things. Pick one. Each has a root, which is where it comes from in our houses. We're not saying the root does anything. We're saying you already know how to do this.

### The hum

*Root:* the sound under the hymn. The hum the mothers of the church make when the choir's between verses, the low note somebody holds when the preacher gets it right. You've heard it your whole life.

*How:* somebody starts a note. Any note. Everybody who wants to joins on whatever note they've got. Hold it as long as your breath goes, breathe, come back in. Three minutes, or until it ends on its own. No words. No song. Just the note.

- Time: 3 minutes.
- Sensory: sound in the chest, a little vibration in the face. Not loud. Eyes open or closed.
- Low-demand: breathe out slow with your mouth closed. That's a hum nobody can hear. Or sit in the room and don't.

### Call and response

*Root:* church, the playground, the cookout, the DJ at the reunion. "Y'all ready?" "Yeah." One voice, then everybody. It's how we've always said *we're all here* without a speech.

*How:* one person has the call, and it's one line. "We made it." Everybody answers with the same word back: "Made it." Do it three times. The caller can change the line ("We're here." / "Here."). Keep it to two or three words each side.

- Time: 2 minutes.
- Sensory: voices, a little loud on the third round. No touch.
- Low-demand: tap the table on the response instead of saying it. Or hold the call and let someone else answer.

### The line dance

*Root:* the Electric Slide, the Cupid Shuffle, the Wobble. Every wedding, every cookout, every reunion. Everybody knows it, and nobody has to look at anybody, because you're all facing the same wall.

*How:* somebody puts the song on. Whoever's getting up gets up. Whoever isn't, doesn't. It ends when the song ends. One song, not a set.

- Time: 5 to 10 minutes.
- Sensory: loud, crowded, people moving close. This is the loudest thing in the kit.
- Low-demand: sit and do the steps with your feet under the table. Or play the song and let the room do what it does. Or leave the room for the length of the song; the exit plan (section 3) covers that.

### The walk after dinner

*Root:* walking a plate to the neighbor. Walking off the food. "Let's go see Miss Pearl." The walk was never about exercise. It was the part of the day where you got out of the house and nobody could ask you anything because you were both looking at the street.

*How:* two or more people. Out the door, around the block or to the corner and back. Talking is allowed and not required. Side by side, not face to face.

- Time: 15 minutes.
- Sensory: cold air, quiet, maybe dark. Wear the coat.
- Low-demand: to the mailbox and back. Or stand on the porch while somebody else walks.

### The porch sit

*Root:* the porch, the stoop, the front step, the folding chair in the carport. Sitting next to somebody and facing out. Nobody has to talk. Nobody has to look at anybody. That's the whole design of a porch.

*How:* two chairs, or a step wide enough for two. Sit. Face the same direction. Ten minutes, or until somebody gets cold.

- Time: 10 minutes.
- Sensory: stillness, cold. Whatever the street sounds like.
- Low-demand: the back step, alone, with the door cracked so you can hear the house.

### The kitchen at 6 a.m.

*Root:* the one who gets up before everybody to put the greens on, to get the turkey in, to make the coffee and not talk to anyone for an hour. That hour is the quietest the house gets all season. It's always been where somebody went to be steady.

*How:* one person or two. Low light. One task with your hands: peeling, stirring, washing a pot. No music, or music low. No talking unless it's about the food.

- Time: 20 to 30 minutes.
- Sensory: warm, quiet, a little dark. Smell of whatever's cooking.
- Low-demand: sit in the kitchen with a cup while somebody else does the hands part. You're still in the kitchen at 6 a.m. That counts.

## 2. The empty chair (a card for grief at the holidays)

Somebody who was at this table isn't. Maybe this year, maybe ten years ago and it's still their chair.

This card doesn't tell you how to do this. Nobody gets to. It gives you choices, and every one of them is right.

**You can:**
- Set their plate, or not.
- Say their name at the table, or not.
- Make their dish, or not. If you make it and it's not the same, it's not supposed to be.
- Leave the chair where it was, or move it, or sit in it.
- Cry at the table, or in the car, or not at all this year.
- Talk about them the whole time, or not once.

**And:**
- You don't have to be okay by dessert.
- You don't have to say "they'd want us to be happy." Maybe they would. You don't have to perform it.
- If two people at the table are doing this differently, both are right. One's setting the plate and one can't look at it. Nobody's grieving wrong.
- The teen who barely knew them and the elder who knew them sixty years don't have to match.

**One thing you can do with your hands, if you want one:** put something of theirs on the table. A serving spoon. A dish towel. The good bowl. Don't announce it. Whoever notices, notices.

- Time: none. This card is a page you read once.
- Sensory: whatever comes up. That's allowed.
- Low-demand: read the first line and stop. "Every one of them is right."

## 3. The exit plan (leaving a room with your dignity on)

Everybody in the house gets this. It's for the teen at a table that isn't safe. It's for the elder who's tired and would rather die than say so. It's for the host at hour six. It works the same for all three, and that's the point: nobody has to explain.

**The house rule.** The host says it once, early, before food:

> "If anybody gets up and leaves the room tonight, nobody follows and nobody asks. That's the rule this year."

Say it plain. Don't say who it's for. It's for everybody, which means nobody's being singled out when they use it.

**Three exits, smallest to biggest.** All of them are always true. Nobody has to prove any of them.

1. **The errand.** "I'm going to get something out of the car." "I'm going to check on the pie." "I'll take the trash." Five minutes, out and back. Nobody has ever been asked why they took the trash out.
2. **The room.** The bathroom, the porch, the back bedroom, the car with the heat on. Fifteen minutes or an hour. The door can be closed.
3. **The leave.** Going home. "I'm going to head out." That's a full sentence. It doesn't need a reason after it.

**Coming back.** When you come back, nobody says "where'd you go" or "you okay?" You sit down. The conversation is wherever it got to. This half of the rule is what makes the first half usable.

**For the host:** put one chair somewhere quieter, before people arrive. A bedroom, the den, the porch if it's not too cold. Don't label it. Don't point it out. People will find it, and the elder who won't say she's tired will end up in it, and that's the whole plan working.

**One optional piece, for anyone who wants a person.** Pick one person in the house before the day. Agree on one text: a single period, or a single word. It means "come get me." That person walks in and says "I need you for a minute" and you leave together. Nobody else knows there was a text. This is optional and it needs one person you trust. If you don't have one in the house that day, the three exits still work alone.

- Time: the rule takes ten seconds to say. The exits take as long as you need.
- Sensory: leaving a loud room for a quieter one. That's the whole mechanic.
- Low-demand: the errand. It's the smallest one and nobody has ever questioned it.

## 4. Soul Compass (the family check-in)

Three questions. Everyone in the house answers, once, sometime during the season. Out loud or on paper. One word each is fine.

1. **What was the steadiest moment this season?**
2. **What was the hardest?**
3. **What do you want to keep for next year?**

**How it's kept.** The answers stay in the house. Write them on the card in the PDF and put the card in a drawer, or say them at the table and let them go. Nobody's answers leave. This isn't a survey. It's the family's own record, and it's yours.

After the season, if the family wants to, you can share what you choose with us: not names, not individual answers, just what the house decided together to pass along. That feeds a season readout that comes back to families. Choosing not to is a full answer, and the kit worked exactly the same.

We don't promise the check-in does anything. It's a record the family keeps. That's the whole claim.

- Time: 5 minutes, once.
- Sensory: talking, or writing. Neither required; a nod at somebody else's answer is a way to be in it.
- Low-demand: answer question three only.

## The bare minimum (low-demand version)

If you have five minutes and nothing left:

1. Say the house rule ("If anybody gets up and leaves the room, nobody follows and nobody asks.").
2. Put the "this isn't therapy" card on the fridge.
3. Know that the errand exit is always available to you, too.

That's a steady house. Everything else in this kit is extra.

## Open questions for the auditors (round 1)

- **Adult:** the six roots. Are these yours? Is there one that reads as a stereotype rather than a house you've been in? Which one would you cut, and what would you put in its place?
- **Adult:** the grief card. Is "you don't have to be okay by dessert" the right register, or too cute for the year somebody actually died? What line would you want on that card?
- **Trans auditors:** the exit plan. Does "nobody follows and nobody asks" hold up at a table where a relative is the problem, or does the host saying the rule out loud draw attention to whoever leaves first? Would you rather the rule not be announced at all, just printed?
- **Trans auditors:** the one-text option. Is one trusted person a real thing at the tables you're thinking of, or does it assume a safety that isn't there? Should it be cut so nobody feels the kit expected them to have somebody?
- **Everyone:** the house rule "anyone can pass" opens the kit. Does it need to be said again before each practice, or does that get preachy?
