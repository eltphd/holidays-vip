## Before you open this (read this first)

This is a cooking kit, built on the food your family already makes at Thanksgiving, the way it's actually made. Nobody's greens are getting corrected. Where a dish has a choice, it's there because somebody at your table might need it, not because the dish needed fixing.

These are practices, not treatment. We tell you where each thing comes from, and we don't promise what it will do. The house rule for everything in this kit: **anyone can pass.** The teen can pass on their dish. Grandma can pass on the archive card. The cook can pass on all of it and just cook.

> **This isn't therapy.** These are practices, not treatment. We name where each one comes from; we don't promise what it will do. If someone at your table needs more than a practice, reach for a professional. If it's heavier than a practice can hold, these are people who pick up: **988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline** — call or text 988, any hour. **Call BlackLine** — 1-800-604-5841, peer support by us, for us, with an LGBTQ+ Black femme lens. **Trans Lifeline** — 877-565-8860, run by trans people, for trans people, weekdays 1–9 PM Eastern.

**What this asks of you**
- Time: 20 minutes to read and plan, a shopping trip, and a cooking day that's going to happen anyway. The kit doesn't add a day; it rearranges the one you've got.
- Sensory: a hot kitchen, a full house, smoked meat and onions and butter in the air for hours, people in and out. If that's a lot for somebody, sections 2 and 3 can happen in another room.
- Low-demand version: "The bare minimum" at the end. It's a real version.

## 1. The dishes, as they're made

Five dishes; make the ones your family makes. Each plan says what it needs from the house and gives one choice, optional every time. Skip it and the dish is right.

**On the day:** the cook is the cook. Everybody else gets a job that isn't "get out of the kitchen": the elder runs the seasoning from a chair, the teen owns one dish (section 4), the little ones wash and taste.

### Greens
*Time:* 20 minutes to clean, 2 to 3 hours on the stove. *Sensory:* the smoked-meat smell fills the house early and stays; cleaning greens is cold and wet on the hands. *Low-demand:* buy the bagged, pre-cut greens. Grandma will say something. Buy them anyway.

Two or three bunches of collards (or collards and mustards, if that's your house), stripped off the stem, torn, washed three times until there's no grit in the sink. A smoked turkey wing or two, or a ham hock, or neck bones, whatever your people use, in a big pot with water, half an onion, a little garlic, a pinch of red pepper. Let the meat go an hour so the water turns into something. Then the greens, in handfuls, until they wilt down and fit. Lid on. Low. Leave them alone. They're done when the pot likker tastes like the meat. Hot sauce and vinegar on the table.

*One choice, if you need it:* if an elder at the table has been told to watch salt, the smoked meat is where the salt lives. Rinse it before it goes in, and don't add salt to the pot. Salt and hot sauce go on the table, and every plate seasons itself. The greens are still the greens.

### Mac and cheese
*Time:* 30 minutes hands-on, 40 in the oven. *Sensory:* hot oven, cheese smell, a lot of grating, and the loudest argument in the kitchen (how many cheeses). *Low-demand:* one cheese, the block kind, and the box of elbows. It'll be right.

Elbows, boiled a minute short of the box. Butter in the hot pasta. Eggs beaten into evaporated milk and regular milk with salt, black pepper, and whatever else your house does. Cheese in layers: a sharp cheddar for sure, then whatever your family swears by. Pasta, cheese, pasta, cheese, milk poured over, cheese on top. Baked until the top is brown at the edges and the middle doesn't jiggle. Let it sit ten minutes. It cuts in squares. That's how you know.

*One choice, if you need it:* if a cousin can't do dairy, don't change the pan. Make a small dish on the side with what they can have, baked next to the big one. Nobody's mac got changed, and the cousin's got theirs.

### Candied yams
*Time:* 15 minutes of peeling, 45 minutes to an hour. *Sensory:* butter and cinnamon and sugar, sticky hands, a hot syrup that spits. Peeling is a good sitting-down job. *Low-demand:* the canned yams, drained, with the same butter and sugar over them. Same smell.

Sweet potatoes peeled and cut in thick rounds. In a pan with a stick of butter, brown sugar and white sugar, cinnamon, a splash of vanilla, nutmeg if your family does that, a little orange juice or water. Covered, on low, until the potatoes are soft and the liquid has gone thick and dark. Spoon the syrup back over them. Marshmallows and a minute under the broiler if that's your house; not, if it's not.

*One choice, if you need it:* there's always one kid who won't touch them once the marshmallows are on. Before the marshmallows, scoop that kid a plain corner into a little bowl. They eat yams. Everybody wins.

### Cornbread dressing
*Time:* the cornbread is a day ahead (30 minutes); the dressing is 30 minutes to put together and an hour to bake. *Sensory:* sage and onion and celery; it's the dish that makes the house smell like Thanksgiving. *Low-demand:* two boxes of cornbread mix, baked the day before. Nobody has ever noticed.

Cornbread, baked a day ahead so it dries a little, crumbled into the biggest bowl you have. Onion and celery cooked soft in butter. Sage. A lot of black pepper. Some houses add torn-up white bread, some add sausage, some add boiled eggs or the chopped giblets. Chicken broth (or turkey broth from the neck) poured in until it's wet, wetter than you think. A couple of eggs beaten in to hold it. Baked until the top is set and brown and the middle isn't soup. Gravy over it.

*One choice, if you need it:* if there's a cousin who doesn't eat meat, pull a small pan aside before the sausage or giblets go in, and wet that pan with vegetable broth. Same cornbread, same sage. It sits next to the big one.

### Sweet potato pie
*Time:* an hour on the stove for the potatoes, 15 minutes to mix, an hour to bake. *Sensory:* the whole house knows when the pie is in; nutmeg, warm sugar. Mashing is a sit-down job. *Low-demand:* a store crust. The filling is the pie.

Sweet potatoes boiled whole in their skins until a fork goes through, peeled while warm, mashed until there are no strings. Butter melted in. Sugar, brown or white or both. Eggs. Evaporated milk. Vanilla, cinnamon, nutmeg; some people put a little lemon in. Whipped smooth, poured into the crust, baked until the middle is set and the top has a crack or two. Cooled all the way before it's cut, which is the hardest part of the day.

*One choice, if you need it:* if somebody at the table has been told by their doctor to go easy on sugar, cut the sugar in the filling by about a third. The pie still sets. It tastes more like the potato. It's a different pie, not a better one, and the family that cuts it that way already knows.

## 2. The "who taught you this" card

One card per dish, four to a print sheet. *Time:* ten minutes, at the table or while the pie cools. *Sensory:* none; it's a pen and a card. *Low-demand:* fill in the first two lines and stop.

```
DISH: ______________________
WHO TAUGHT ME: ______________________
WHO TAUGHT THEM: ______________________
WHAT THEY DID THAT'S NOT IN ANY RECIPE:
______________________________________
WHAT I CHANGED, AND WHY:
______________________________________
WHO I'M TEACHING: ______________________
```

The fourth line is the one that matters. Every dish has a thing the person who taught you did with their hands that never got written down: the way she tested the greens with her fingers, the second stick of butter when nobody was looking, the one pan the yams have to go in. Write that. The names matter too, but the fourth line is the recipe.

The last line is for whoever's next. If it's the teen, put the teen. If it's nobody yet, leave it and keep the card. If the person who taught you has passed, the card is still theirs, and the dish on the menu carries their name: "Big Mama's dressing (by Renee)."

**After:** the kit's back pocket, a kitchen drawer, a photo on a phone. Somewhere the next cook will find it.

## 3. The week card (water and sleep)

The plainest card in the kit. It's Thanksgiving week: the house is full, the cook's been on her feet since Tuesday, the kids are up past anything. Here's what people who've run a full house for fifty years do. None of it is a rule; there are no numbers on this card on purpose. *Time:* none; it's the week you're already having. *Low-demand:* one cup and one nap.

**Water.**
- A cup of water on the counter next to the coffee pot. Not a rule, a location. If it's there, people drink it.
- The cook forgets to drink. The youngest one who wants a job hands the cook a cup every so often. Real job, real title: the cup runner.
- The elder's cup lives where the elder sits. Whoever walks by refills it.

**Sleep.**
- The cook goes to bed on time the night before. That's the one thing on this card the house should protect. Somebody else finishes the dishes Wednesday.
- Naps are allowed on Thanksgiving. The couch after the plate is a tradition, not a failure.
- A quiet hour at some point, if the house can hold one. TV lower, kids in one room, elders in another. *Sensory note:* this is the hour for whoever's had enough noise.

That's the card. It's what the aunties did; that's the root we're naming.

## 4. The grocery list, split by generation

One page, four columns, one generation each. It splits the work the way a big family already splits it, and makes the split visible so nobody's carrying the whole thing alone. *Time:* ten minutes to fill in, then the store. *Sensory:* the store that week is loud and crowded; one person can shop and the others hand over their columns. *Low-demand:* the elder's column can say "I'm bringing the pie" and nothing else.

```
ELDER              ADULT              TEEN               LITTLE ONES
(the dish that's   (the big ones:     (one dish, mine:   (what they wash,
theirs by right)   bird, dressing,    _____________)     what they taste,
                   greens, mac)                          what they carry)
___________        ___________        ___________        ___________
___________        ___________        ___________        ___________
```

**The teen's column is the point of this page.** The teen picks a dish, from section 1 or their own, and it's theirs: their column, their store run (or their list handed to whoever's going), their pan, their spot on the stove, their name on the menu. Nobody hovers. The elder sits nearby and answers questions if asked; that's the elder's whole job on that dish. If it comes out wrong, it goes on the table anyway, and next year it comes out right. That's how everybody learned.

**The pass.** If the teen doesn't want a dish this year, they say so, and somebody else makes it, no speech. The teen can take the cup runner job, or the archive cards, or nothing. Next year the column's still there.

**Ujima, if you want to name it.** Collective work: the kitchen is everybody's, and the list makes that true on paper.

## The bare minimum (low-demand version)

If you've got the energy for the cooking and nothing else, do these three:

1. Make the dishes the way you always make them. Ignore every "one choice" unless you need one.
2. Fill in the first two lines of one archive card (the dish, who taught you). Put it in the drawer.
3. Put a cup of water next to the coffee pot.

That's the kit. The rest is extra, and the extra is real.

## Open questions for the auditors (round 1)

- Older adult: are the dishes written the way you'd say them? What would you never leave out of the greens or the dressing? Is "rinse the smoked meat" something you'd do, or a stranger telling you how to cook?
- Older adult: the archive card. Would you fill it in, or is it homework?
- Adult: the grocery split. Does a four-column list match how your house divides Thanksgiving, or is it more structure than real?
- Adult: the pie choice ("cut the sugar by a third") is the adaptation closest to the line. Does it read as a choice for a specific person, or as diet talk? If it creeps, we cut it and the pie gets no choice.
- Adolescent (reader, not owner): would you take the dish? Is "your name on the menu" a reason to, or a reason not to?
- Everyone: the week card. Anything that reads as a rule instead of a habit, or sounds like a pamphlet?
