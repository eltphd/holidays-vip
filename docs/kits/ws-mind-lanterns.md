## Before you open this (read this first)

This is a games kit for one night of Kwanzaa week, or New Year's, when everybody's in the living room anyway and the elders have sat through the young people's music for three days. The games are ours: the card table, the sayings, the stories told every year with one detail different. Two are rigged for the elders, on purpose.

These are practices, not treatment. Games night is a games night. We're not saying it does anything for anybody's brain, because we don't know that, and neither does anybody selling you a puzzle app. We tell you where each game comes from. We don't promise what it will do. The house rule for everything in here: **anyone can pass.** The elder can sit out a round. The teen can hand the eighth night back. Nobody plays who doesn't want to.

> **This isn't therapy.** These are practices, not treatment. We name where each one comes from; we don't promise what it will do. If someone at your table needs more than a practice, reach for a professional. If it's heavier than a practice can hold, these are people who pick up: **988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline** — call or text 988, any hour. **Call BlackLine** — 1-800-604-5841, peer support by us, for us, with an LGBTQ+ Black femme lens. **Trans Lifeline** — 877-565-8860, run by trans people, for trans people, weekdays 1–9 PM Eastern.

**What this asks of you**
- Time: one evening, about two hours, and it can be one. The eighth-night puzzle is a separate night the teen runs.
- Sensory: a full living room, people talking over each other, cards slapped on a table. Pass the Beat is clapping and gets loud. No touch beyond cards. Keep a chair by the door for whoever needs the edge of the room.
- Low-demand version: "The bare minimum" at the end. It's one game and a bowl.

## 1. Games night

Five games; play two or three. Before Your Time and Finish the Saying are built so the elders win; the card table is built so they win and teach; the other two are anybody's. Keep score if that's your house. **Who runs it:** anyone but the cook. Give the teen the sheet; they'll run it faster than you.

### Game 1 — Before Your Time (elders win)
*Time:* 20 to 30 minutes. *Sensory:* talking, laughing; quiet enough to hear the elder. *Low-demand:* the elder tells one story; nobody votes.

The elders hold the deck, because the deck is their memory. Each elder, on their turn, says one thing about the world before the youngest person in the room was born. A price, a street, a rule, a song, a thing the family did. It's true or it's made up. Everybody else votes. The elder gets a point for every person they fool; each guesser gets a point for calling it right.

Sample turns: "Your grandfather and I met at a bus stop on Auburn Avenue." "Bread was a nickel when I was a girl." "This family did not eat mac and cheese at Thanksgiving until 1974." One of those is made up. The elder knows which.

*Why the elders win:* they hold the facts, and they know exactly which detail sounds wrong and isn't. *Where it's from:* every porch.

### Game 2 — Finish the Saying (elders win)
*Time:* 15 minutes. *Sensory:* fast, call-and-response, some shouting. *Low-demand:* the elder says the sayings; nobody has to finish them.

Somebody reads the first half of a saying; first to finish it gets the point. The sayings are the ones the elders raised everybody on. The kit sheet has forty; the elders will add more.

The shape: "A hard head makes a ___." "Don't let your mouth write a check ___." "Every shut eye ___." "God don't like ___."

*Why the elders win:* they've said every one of these a thousand times, usually at the people they're now playing against. The teens know half. The little ones know the ones said to them this week.

*The flip:* last round, the teens say a saying from their world and the elders finish it. They won't. That's the round the teens win, and it's one round.

### Game 3 — The Card Table (elders win and teach)
*Time:* an hour, or until somebody goes to bed. *Sensory:* cards slapped down, trash talk, four people close at a table. *Low-demand:* the youngest sits beside the elder holding the cards. No rules; just holding.

Spades or bid whist, whichever your house plays. The one rule this kit adds: **every elder partners with the youngest person who can hold a hand.** The elder bids; the young one plays the cards the elder points to; halfway through the night the elder stops pointing and lets them go. That's the teaching. The other pair is whoever's left, and they'll lose.

The kit sheet has both games' rules on one page. If an elder plays bid whist, that elder runs the table and the sheet stays in the drawer.

*Why the elders win:* fifty years at the table. *Where it's from:* the same table. It's Ujima with a deck of cards: the elder's hand carries the kid's, and by the end of the night the kid's hand carries its own.

### Game 4 — Bring It Back (anybody's)
*Time:* 15 minutes. *Sensory:* everybody talking in turn; quiet listening. *Low-demand:* a chain of three instead of the whole circle.

The old memory chain, with a family shape. First person: "I'm going to Big Mama's house and I'm bringing the greens." Next person repeats and adds: "...the greens and a folding chair." Around it goes. Miss one, you're out. Last one in wins. Every item has to be something actually carried into that house, which is where the arguments start ("Nobody ever brought a ham." "Your uncle brought a ham in 1998.").

*Where it's from:* the back seat, on long drives. Kids usually win this one. Let them.

### Game 5 — Pass the Beat (anybody's)
*Time:* 10 minutes. *Sensory:* loud; clapping, knee-slapping, feet. Skip it if you've had enough noise; play it if you need to move. *Low-demand:* clap along without being in the chain.

One person claps a short pattern (two claps, a knee slap). The next person repeats it and adds one beat. Around the circle the pattern grows. Whoever breaks it is out, and it starts over from them. Elders who came up on hand games ("Miss Mary Mack," "Down Down Baby") hang in longer than they expect. Teens tend to win; their hands are fast. Sometimes the little ones win because they're not thinking.

*Where it's from:* the playground, the church steps, the porch.

## 2. The brain myths card

One card, read out loud between games and argued about. It's things people say about the brain, and what's not known. It doesn't tell you what's true; it tells you what's not settled, so nobody's running the family on a saying. *Time:* five minutes. *Sensory:* none. *Low-demand:* read one line.

**Things people say, and what's not known:**

- *"Crosswords keep your mind sharp."* Doing crosswords makes you better at crosswords. Whether it does anything past that, nobody's settled. Do them if you like them.
- *"You lose it when you get old."* Forgetting a name isn't a verdict, and "old" isn't one thing. Somebody's grandaddy is going to beat the whole room at Finish the Saying tonight. That's not a claim; that's a score.
- *"You only use ten percent of your brain."* Nobody's found the ninety percent that's off. This one's just said.
- *"Fish is brain food."* Grandma said it. Nobody's settled it. Eat the fish because it's good.
- *"If you forget where you put your keys, that's the start of it."* Everybody loses keys. The teen loses keys.
- *"Games like these keep Grandma sharp."* Honest answer: we don't know that, and this kit doesn't say it. What this kit says is she'll win.

**The last line on the card:** if somebody in this house has noticed a real change in somebody they love, not a lost key, a change, that's a conversation with a doctor, not with a card. The card's here so nobody uses a saying in place of that conversation, in either direction.

## 3. The phone-down hour

*Time:* one hour. It can be forty minutes. *Sensory:* the room gets quieter than people expect and then louder than people expect. *Low-demand:* twenty minutes, or phone face-down in your pocket counts as down.

**Who sets it:** the teen, not the parent. The teen picks the hour, announces it, and runs the bowl. Set by a parent, a phone-down hour is a punishment; set by a teen, it's a game. Same hour.

**The bowl.** A bowl, a basket, a pot with a lid. Everybody's phone goes in, including the elder who "doesn't even use mine" and the parent who's "just checking on the roast." The teen's goes in first, on top, where everybody can see it.

**Exceptions, said out loud before the hour:** the cousin on call, the one waiting on a ride, the one whose kid is with a sitter. They keep the phone face-down beside them, and nobody asks twice. Anyone can pass on the hour; they sit with the room and keep their phone. No speech.

**What fills the hour:** the games. Without them it's an hour of people looking at the bowl.

**When it ends:** the teen calls it. Phones come back. Nobody mentions what anybody missed.

*Where it's from:* the living room before there were phones in it. Every elder remembers this hour without a bowl. Ask them; that's a Before Your Time turn.

## 4. The eighth night: the teen's puzzle

Kwanzaa is seven nights. The eighth, the night after Imani or whichever night the family picks, is the teen's to design (Teen Room says the same). This kit gives them one way to spend it, if they want it: a puzzle about this family, built by the teen, for the family to solve.

**This is a template, not a puzzle.** The teen writes the puzzle. Nobody edits it. If it's too hard, it's too hard. If it's too easy, the elders will say so, and they'll be lying.

*Time:* an hour or two for the teen to build it alone; 30 to 45 minutes for the family to solve it. *Sensory:* the teen picks. A house-wide hunt is moving and loud; a table quiz is sitting and quiet. *Low-demand:* three clues instead of five, or one question the family answers together. Or the pass.

**The template (a two-page fill-in in the kit):**

```
THE EIGHTH NIGHT PUZZLE
by: ___________________

WHAT KIND (pick one, or make one up):
  [ ] a hunt through the house
  [ ] a "who said it" quiz
  [ ] a code the family has to crack
  [ ] a crossword of family words
  [ ] ___________________

THE FIVE CLUES (three is fine):
  1. Clue: ____________________  Answer: __________  Who'd know it: __________
  (2 through 5 the same)

THE RULE (what the family has to do to win):
  ______________________________________________

THE PRIZE (doesn't have to be a thing):
  ______________________________________________

MY TEAM (if I want one):
  ______________________________________________

IF I'M PASSING, here's what I'd rather do on the eighth night:
  ______________________________________________
```

**Notes to the teen, printed on the back:**
- "Who'd know it" is the column that makes the puzzle work. Point every clue at a different person in the house, so the elder knows one answer, the little cousin knows another, and nobody can win without asking. That's Kuumba, if you want the word: you're making the thing that makes them need each other.
- The prize can be "I don't have to do dishes New Year's Day." Everybody will agree to it.
- If you build it and don't want to run it, hand the sheet to somebody and sit in the audience.
- The pass line at the bottom is real. Fill in that line and nothing else, and the eighth night is whatever you wrote there. The kit did its job.

## The bare minimum (low-demand version)

If you've got one hour and half the energy:

1. The bowl. Phones in, teen calls it, forty minutes.
2. One game: Finish the Saying. The elder reads the first half; whoever wants to finishes them. No score.
3. That's it. The eighth night can be next year's.

That's a games night. Everything else in the kit is extra.

## Open questions for the auditors (round 1)

- Older adult: do Before Your Time and Finish the Saying feel rigged for you, or like a kid's game with your name on it? Which sayings are missing?
- Older adult: the card table rule (elder partners with the youngest). Is that how you'd teach, or is it taking your real partner away? Spades or bid whist for the sheet?
- Older adult: the brain myths card. Is any line disrespectful? Does the doctor line land, or does it feel like the kit sneaking a worry in?
- Adolescent: would you set the phone-down hour, or is "the teen runs the bowl" a job you'd be embarrassed to be handed?
- Adolescent: the eighth-night template. Too many boxes? Which would you skip? Would you use the pass line, or just not fill in the sheet?
- Everyone: "Games night the elders win." Is it a line you'd say out loud, or does it make the young people not want to play?
