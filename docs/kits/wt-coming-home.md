# Coming Home — draft 1 (for audit round 1)

**Category:** Whole Table · **Format:** practice · **Kit id:** `wt-coming-home` · **Price:** $39 digital · **Files by:** Dec 1
**Who it's for:** the grown child coming home for the holidays, and the parent (or the grandmother, or the auntie who raised them) receiving them.
**One line:** Two mirrored decks for the one returning and the ones receiving.

**Owners for audit:** lesbians (the returning deck, the boundaries card) · adolescent (the sibling card, the teen voice throughout) · adult (the receiving deck, the repair script).

---

## Before you open this (read this first)

This kit doesn't ask anyone to come out, be outed, or explain themselves. Not the one coming home, not the one opening the door, not the little brother watching from the couch. The cards work for whatever you're carrying up the steps: who you love, what you're called now, a divorce, a year of not calling, a job that fell through. The cards don't ask. They make room.

There are two decks, one for each side of the door. Each one works alone. You can do yours without the other person ever knowing there's a deck. Nobody has to agree to anything first.

It shipped to you in discreet mode. The email said "Your holidayz.vip kit." The file is named `holidayz-winter-kit-02.pdf`. The cover reads as a winter kit. If you're going home to a house that isn't safe yet, or you're a teen living in one, you can own this and nobody learns anything from it.

**What this asks of you**
- Time: about five minutes a card, ten cards. One a night across Kwanzaa, or the whole deck in 40 minutes on the drive.
- Sensory: nothing loud, no required touch. Reading and thinking. Writing is optional.
- Low-demand version: skip to "The bare minimum" at the end. It's a real version, not a consolation.

## 1. The two decks (Returning and Receiving)

**How the decks work.** Ten cards each. Same numbers, same subjects, two sides of the door. The returning person reads theirs on the way there. The receiving person reads theirs the week before. Neither deck is a script. A card is something you sit with, and maybe hand across the table. Most people won't. The deck still worked.

The house rule is on the back of every card, both decks: **anyone can pass.**

Cards 1, 2, and 10 are door cards. The seven in the middle are the Nguzo Saba, one a night if you're home for Kwanzaa, or in any order if you're not.

**Card 1 — The drive**
*Returning:* What are you carrying up those steps that nobody asked you to carry? Name one thing you can leave in the car.
*Receiving:* What are you hoping they'll bring through the door, and what are you afraid they'll bring? Say both before the bell rings. Then open the door for the person, not the fear.

**Card 2 — The first hour**
*Returning:* What's the first thing you want to hear when you walk in? It's allowed to be "you hungry?" or nothing but your name.
*Receiving:* What's the first thing you're going to say? Make it about them being here, not how long it's been. "How long has it been" is a bill. "Look at you" is a welcome.

**Card 3 — Umoja (Unity)**
*Returning:* Who at this table is already on your side, even if they've never said it? Sit near them.
*Receiving:* Who at this table is already on their side? You don't have to be the only one. Two of you is a table, not a standoff.

**Card 4 — Kujichagulia (Self-Determination)**
*Returning:* What do you want to be called in this house this week? You can say it, or you can just answer to it and let people catch up.
*Receiving:* What do they go by now? If you're not sure, you can ask once: "What do you want me to call you?" If they say "same as always," that's an answer. Then do that.

**Card 5 — Ujima (Collective Work and Responsibility)**
*Returning:* What's one job in this house you're taking off somebody's hands this week? Dishes count. Driving Grandma to church counts.
*Receiving:* What's one job you're handing them, so they're family and not company? Company gets a plate. Family gets the greens to pick.

**Card 6 — Ujamaa (Cooperative Economics)**
*Returning:* What did this house give you that you're still spending? A recipe, a way of talking, a stubbornness. Tell somebody before you leave, or leave the card on the counter.
*Receiving:* What did they bring home that this house didn't have before? A person, a skill, a dish, a word. Ask to be taught.

**Card 7 — Nia (Purpose)**
*Returning:* Why did you come? Not the obligation. The real reason. Keep it where you can reach it on day three.
*Receiving:* Why did you want them here? Tell them one version of it that isn't "because it's the holidays."

**Card 8 — Kuumba (Creativity)**
*Returning:* What's one thing you'd change about how this family does the holidays? You get to propose it. They get to say no.
*Receiving:* What's one thing about the holidays you'd let them redesign? The eighth night, the music, who says grace. Offer it before they ask.

**Card 9 — Imani (Faith)**
*Returning:* What do you believe about this family that you'd like to be true by next year? Believe it this week.
*Receiving:* What do you believe about them that you haven't said in a while? Say it before they go. "I'm proud of you" doesn't need a reason attached.

**Card 10 — The last morning**
*Returning:* What are you taking back that you didn't bring? What are you leaving here on purpose?
*Receiving:* What did you learn this week that you want to still know in March? Write it down. March is a long way off.

**The sibling card (for the teen at the table).** One extra card, for the younger brother or sister. It says: You're not the referee. You're not the translator. If it gets loud, you can say "anyone can pass," take your plate, and go. That's not rude. That's the rule. And one question, for you only: What's one thing about them that the grown folks don't get and you do? You don't have to tell anybody. Just know you know.

*Time:* five minutes a card. *Sensory:* quiet; nobody reads aloud unless they want to. *Low-demand:* cards 2 and 10 only. That's a full deck.

## 2. The boundaries card (peer register)

One card, two sides. It's a card because a boundary is a sentence, not a paragraph. If it takes a paragraph, it's an argument.

**Front (returning).** Fill in the blanks before you get there:

> "I'm here till ______. I'm not doing the ______ conversation this trip. If it starts, I'm going to go for a walk and come back. That's not leaving. That's a walk."

Say it once, early, to one person you trust. Then somebody in the house knows what the walk is when it happens.

**Back (receiving).** Fill in the blanks before they get there:

> "This is my house and everybody in it is welcome, including the ones I'm still learning. If somebody has a problem with that, they take it up with me, in the kitchen, not at the table."

Say it once, before the day, to the relative most likely to need it. Say it like "take your shoes off." It's a house rule, not a position.

**A boundary is not a request for agreement.** It's real when you've said it and you'll do what you said. Mama said "because I said so" and it held for thirty years. You get the same authority over your own house, or your own two feet.

**For the teen.** Your boundary can be one word: "Pass." You don't owe the table a reason.

*Time:* ten minutes to fill in, once. *Sensory:* none. *Low-demand:* skip the blanks. Just know which conversation you're not having.

## 3. The repair script (doesn't require agreement first)

Something went sideways. A word, a look, a name said wrong. The repair is for the next morning, or the next hour. It's short, because long is a debate, and it works with one person doing it. You don't need them to agree about what happened. You need them at the table tomorrow.

Four lines. Say the ones you can. Either side can go first.

**1. Name that it went bad, not whose fault.**
> "Last night went bad. I don't want it to stay bad."

**2. Say what you want.**
> "I want you at this table tomorrow."

**3. Put the argument down without settling it.**
> "We don't have to agree about it tonight. We might not agree about it ever. I still want you here."

**4. One small next thing.**
> "Come help me with the greens."

Under a minute. The greens do more work than the words. Hands busy, side by side, no eye contact required. That's how this family always made up.

**The "I'm not there yet" line.** If you can't say the four, say this one:
> "I'm not ready to talk about it. I'm still glad you came." (Or: "I'm still glad I came.")

That's a repair, too. It doesn't fix the thing that was said. It gets everybody back at the table with the thing unfixed, which is how most families have survived most things.

*Time:* one minute. *Sensory:* say it in the kitchen, low voice, not across a room. *Low-demand:* line 4 only. "Come help me with the greens" is a complete repair.

## 4. "Leave early with love" (the exit plan)

Leaving is not failing. This family has a hundred years of people who left a room to keep the peace and came back the next day. The plan makes leaving something you decided, not something that happened to you.

**Decide three things on the drive (returning):**
- *The day.* When you're leaving if everything goes fine. Say it early: "I'm here till Sunday." A known end makes the middle easier.
- *The line.* The one thing that means you're leaving early. Not a list. You'll know it.
- *The ride.* Your own car, keys in your pocket. A cousin who'll drive. A bus ticket you already bought. A friend's couch. All plans.

**The halfway exit.** Most nights you don't need the door. You need the porch. Twenty minutes outside, a bedroom with the door shut, a walk to the store for nothing. Tell one person: "I'm stepping out. I'm coming back." Then come back. This is the one you'll use nine times out of ten.

**The full exit.** If it's the line, it's the line. One sentence, to whoever's closest:
> "I'm going to head out. I love y'all. I'll call Sunday."

No reasons. No speech. Take a plate if it's offered. Go. Then call Sunday, because the call is what turns leaving early into leaving with love.

**For the one receiving.** Your whole job: make them a plate, say "text me when you're home," and don't chase them to the car with the argument. When they call Sunday, pick up. A door you have to earn your way back through is a wall.

**For the teen.** If your sibling leaves the house, you don't have to pick a side. "They needed to go" is a complete sentence.

*Time:* three decisions on the drive; one sentence at the door. *Sensory:* the porch is quieter than the kitchen, and it counts. *Low-demand:* know your ride. That's the whole plan.

## 5. Soul Compass check-in (both sides)

A short check-in the family keeps. It isn't a test, and nobody's answers leave the house. It's a gift the family gives itself: a few words about how this year went, so next year starts from something real.

**How it works.** Three questions a side, answered alone, on paper or in your head, when the visit starts. Ask the same three again when it ends, past tense. Then one shared question, if you want.

**Returning:**
- How much of me is walking in the door? (a little / some / most / all of me)
- Who in this house do I expect to see me?
- What would make this week easier than last year?

**Receiving:**
- How much of them am I ready to see? (some / most / all of them)
- What did I do right last time that I want to do again?
- What's one thing I want to do better?

**The shared question (optional):** One word for this visit. Put it on the fridge, or keep it. The words can go in the kitchen drawer with last year's.

**What happens with it.** It stays with you. If the household chooses to share the fridge words after the season (the words, not the cards), that consented share feeds a readout that comes back to the family. Nothing individual, nothing named.

*Time:* three minutes, twice. *Sensory:* none; the bathroom counts as the private room. *Low-demand:* the one word. Skip everything else.

## 6. The bare minimum (low-demand version)

If you have ten minutes and no energy, do these three and nothing else:

1. Read card 2 (the first hour) on your side.
2. Know your boundary sentence, even if you never say it.
3. Know your exit sentence and your ride.

That's coming home. Everything else in this kit is extra.

## Open questions for the auditors (round 1)

- Lesbians: card 4 (what do you want to be called) and card 6 ("a person, a skill, a dish, a word") never say the thing plainly. Right amount of unsaid, or should the deck say it once, somewhere? And "that's not leaving, that's a walk": would you say it, or would a workbook?
- Adolescent: the sibling card. Would you be embarrassed to be handed it? Does "you're not the referee" feel like permission or like being told what to do? Does anything in the returning deck ask your sibling to disclose?
- Adult: receiving card 4 says "ask once." Too much pressure on the parent, or not enough? And repair line 3 ("we might not agree about it ever"): honest, or giving up?
- Everyone: the Soul Compass "how much of me" scale. A real question, or does it feel like grading the family? What would you ask instead?
