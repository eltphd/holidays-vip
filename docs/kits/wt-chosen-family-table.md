## Before you open this (read this first)

Chosen family is the family. That's the whole premise. The people at this table chose each other and kept choosing. Some also have blood relatives at another table tonight. Some don't, or can't, or won't this year. Nobody has to say which. Nobody has to explain why they're here, who they came with, or who they didn't go home to. This kit doesn't ask anyone to come out, be outed, or explain themselves. It sets the table.

The hosts might be a married couple. They might be four friends who split the groceries and one of them has the big kitchen. Where this kit says "hosts," read it as whoever's running the room.

It shipped to you in discreet mode. The email said "Your holidayz.vip kit." The file is named `holidayz-winter-kit-03.pdf`. The cover reads as a winter kit. If somebody at your table lives in a house that isn't safe yet, they can carry this home.

**What this asks of you**
- Time: about two hours of prep across the week, and the feast itself, three to five hours.
- Sensory: a full table, so it's loud. One quiet minute (the candle), one loud minute (the toast), music at a level the hosts set, candles optional. No required touch. The photo is opt-in, every time.
- Low-demand version: skip to "The bare minimum" at the end. It's a real version, not a consolation.

## 1. Feast run-of-show

This is for four to sixteen people, afternoon into night. If it's December 31, this is your Karamu, the feast at the end of Kwanzaa, and you're in a long line of Black tables doing exactly this. Any other night, it's still a feast. Shift the times to your day.

**The kitchen is everybody's (Ujima).** The hosts do not cook alone. Everybody coming gets a dish or a job before the day. A dish can be a pot of greens or a bag of ice. A job can be the door, the music, the elder, the dishes, the kids, the ride home. "I can't cook" gets you the door. "I can't afford a dish" gets you the door too, and nobody has to say it; the hosts just assign the door.

**Two hosts, two jobs.** One on the food, one on the room. The food host doesn't leave the kitchen to settle anything. The room host doesn't leave the room to stir anything. Swap at halftime. A crew hosting: each owns a stretch of the evening. Nobody owns the whole night.

| When | What | Who |
|---|---|---|
| The week before | Dishes and jobs assigned. Names drawn (section 3). Keeper picked (section 4). | Hosts |
| Day before | Candle out. Playlist queued. Archive card printed. | Room host |
| 1 hour before | Kitchen host cooking. Room host sets the table. | Both |
| Arrivals | Music on. Coats taken, drinks pointed at. Nobody is introduced as anything but their name. | Door |
| Everybody's here | The welcome, below. Thirty seconds. | Room host |
| Eat | Eat. Talk. Nothing scheduled. | Everybody |
| Between plates | The glass goes around (section 2). | Whoever has the glass |
| After the main plates | The exchange (section 3). | Everybody who brought something |
| Before dessert | The candle (section 5). One minute. | Room host, or the elder |
| Dessert | Photo, for the people who want one (section 4). Archive card goes around. | Keeper |
| Whenever | Plates go home. One extra plate goes to somebody who couldn't come (section 5). | Kitchen host |
| After | Hosts sit down. Somebody else does the dishes. | Not the hosts |

**The welcome.** Thirty seconds, in your own words:

> "Everybody here is family because we said so. Some of y'all I've known twenty years. Some of y'all came with somebody I've known twenty years. Same thing. Say your name when you sit down. That's the only rule. Well, that and anyone can pass."

**If there's an elder.** Chosen family has elders too: the auntie who isn't anybody's aunt, the one who hosted before you had a kitchen. Good chair, first turn with the glass (section 2), and somebody's job is them: the plate, the ride, the volume.

*Time:* two hours of prep across the week; the night runs itself. *Sensory:* arrivals are the loudest stretch; the room host can turn the music down. *Low-demand:* assign dishes and jobs. Skip the table. That's a feast.

## 2. The toast-and-story ritual ("one story, one glass")

One glass goes around the table. Not down the table: around it, to whoever the story was about.

Whoever starts holds the glass and tells one story about somebody at the table. Not about themselves. Sixty seconds. The story is how that person became family: the first time they showed up, the thing they said, the year they carried you. Then they raise the glass to that person and hand it over. That person tells a story about somebody else, and the glass moves again.

**The rules.**
- The story is about somebody else. That's what makes it a toast and not a speech.
- Sixty seconds. If it's going long, the table is allowed to say "and?" and laugh.
- When the glass comes to you, tell a story or just raise it. "I'll just raise the glass" is a full turn. Anyone can pass.
- It stops when it stops. Nobody's owed a turn and nobody's denied one.

**Story starters, for the card in the middle of the table:**
- "The first time I knew you were mine was..."
- "The time you showed up when you didn't have to..."
- "The thing you say that I've started saying..."

**Why this and not "what are you thankful for."** That question makes people talk about themselves and go quiet about the hard year. A story about somebody else is easier to tell and better to hear, and it says out loud, in front of witnesses, how each person got here. Blood family gets that from the photo albums. Chosen family has to say it. Umoja and Imani, at the same time.

**If there's an elder, they start.** Best story, least patience for going long.

*Time:* one to two minutes a person, however many go. *Sensory:* the loud minute is the glass going up; the rest is one voice at a time. *Low-demand:* one toast from a host, no stories: "To the family we chose." Glass up, that's all.

## 3. The gift exchange that doesn't assume money (Ujamaa)

Chosen family runs on uneven money. Somebody at this table is doing fine and somebody is deciding between the dish and the gas to get here. The exchange is built so nobody can tell which is which. That's the fourth principle: what we have, we have between us.

**The one rule.** Nothing bought new after the invitation went out. Everything you bring is one of these:
- *Something you already own* that somebody at this table has admired. The scarf they always borrow. The cast iron you've got two of.
- *Something you can do.* Written on a card, redeemable in the new year: a haircut, a ride to the airport, a Saturday of moving boxes, a night of babysitting.
- *Something you made or found.* A recipe written out by hand. A photo printed from your phone. A playlist. A letter.
- *A plate.* What you cooked, packed to go home with somebody. That's a gift. It always was.

**How it goes.** Draw names the week before. Or skip the names and do the table exchange: everything in the middle wrapped in whatever (a dish towel counts), everybody picks one, anyone can trade once. Names is warmer. The table is easier. Hosts pick.

**What the rule does.** The person with the most money can't bring the biggest thing, because there is no biggest thing. The card that says "I'll drive you to the airport in January" is the best gift at the table, because it is.

**If you really want to buy something.** Buy it in January. Tonight, bring the card.

*Time:* twenty minutes at the table. *Sensory:* wrapping is optional; a dish towel is wrapping. *Low-demand:* bring a card with one sentence on it. "I owe you a Saturday." That's a gift.

## 4. The photo-and-archive prompt (the record)

Blood families have the album, the Bible with the names in the front, the cousin who knows everybody's birthday. Chosen family usually has a group chat that gets lost when somebody changes phones. This is one page, one photo, and one person, so twenty years from now somebody can answer: who was at this table?

**The keeper.** Pick one person the week before. Not a host; hosts have enough. The keeper owns the archive card for the night and gets a copy to everybody who wants one after.

**The archive card.** One page. Print it, or write it on the back of the menu:
- The date, the place (the city is enough), and whose table it was.
- Who came. First names, or the name they go by at this table. Anyone can leave their name off, and the keeper doesn't ask why.
- Who cooked what, and who taught them.
- One line each, optional, on the year: what changed, what held.
- Who couldn't come (first names, or "a few people we love"). See section 5.

**The photo.** One photo, at dessert, for the people who want to be in it. Two versions, always:
- *Faces.* Everybody in it wants to be in it. The keeper asks, "Who's in this one?" and the people who step in are in.
- *No faces.* Hands on the table. The spread. Shoes by the door. Everybody can be in this one, and it's usually the better picture.

**The rule about where it goes.** The photo does not go online unless everybody in it said yes, tonight, out loud. Not "I'll ask later." Not "they won't mind." Somebody at this table has a job, a family, or a landlord who doesn't know they were here, and the picture is theirs to keep off the internet.

**Why this matters (Kuumba).** We make the record. A photo of hands on a table with the date on the back is an heirloom.

*Time:* ten minutes for the card, two for the photo. *Sensory:* one flash or no flash; the no-faces version is quiet and quick. *Low-demand:* the keeper writes the date and the first names on a napkin. That's an archive.

## 5. "The ones who couldn't come" (the candle moment)

Before dessert. One candle, in the middle of the table. Battery is fine. The room host, or the elder, lights it and says:

> "This one's for the ones who couldn't come."

Then a minute of quiet, or as much as the table can hold. Then dessert.

**Who the candle is for.** Nobody has to say. But it's for all of these:
- The ones working tonight.
- The ones who couldn't afford the trip.
- The ones at another table, because that's where they're wanted this year.
- The ones at another table, because this one isn't the one they can be at yet.
- The ones we're not speaking to, and the ones not speaking to us.
- The ones who've died.

You don't say which one is yours. Say a name, or don't. Hold the quiet, or pass the pie. Anyone can pass, including on the quiet: if a minute of silence is too long, step out and come back for dessert.

**The plate that goes out (Nia).** After the candle, the kitchen host makes up one extra plate. It goes to somebody who couldn't come, tomorrow, or home with whoever needs a second one. Either way, the table fed one more person than it seated. That's what the fifth principle looks like on a Tuesday.

**If somebody cries.** Pass the napkins and the pie. Don't fix it.

**Kids at the table.** A kid can light the candle. A kid who can't hold a quiet minute gets the job of bringing out dessert when the minute's up.

*Time:* one minute, two with the plate. *Sensory:* the quietest moment of the night, and the only one; one flame or one light. *Low-demand:* light it, say the line, pass the pie. Skip the quiet.

## 6. The bare minimum (low-demand version)

If you have one hour to plan and no energy, do these three and nothing else:

1. Everybody brings a dish or takes a job. The hosts don't cook alone.
2. One toast, from a host: "To the family we chose."
3. One candle: "This one's for the ones who couldn't come."

That's a chosen family table. Everything else in this kit is extra.

## Open questions for the auditors (round 1)

- Married gay men: the two-host split (food, room, swap at halftime). Is that how your house runs a feast, or how a magazine thinks it runs? And the glass rule, "the story has to be about somebody else": would your table do it, or stall at the second person?
- Lesbians: "nothing bought new after the invitation went out." Too strict? Does it shame the person who wanted to buy something nice? Would a dollar cap be better, or does a cap bring the money back in? And the keeper: one person holding the archive, or should it belong to everybody from the start?
- Everyone: the candle list. "The ones at another table, because this one isn't the one they can be at yet." Too pointed for a mixed table, or the line that makes the candle honest?
- Everyone: the photo rule (no posting unless everybody said yes, out loud, tonight) is stricter than most people's habits. Will it get followed? What would make it?
