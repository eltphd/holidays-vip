# Elder Light — draft 1 (for audit round 1)

**Category:** Whole Table · **Format:** practice · **Kit id:** `wt-elder-light` · **Price:** $29 digital · **Files by:** Dec 12
**Who it's for:** an older LGBTQIA+ adult, and the younger people who love them. Either one can buy it. Either one can open it first.
**One line:** Legacy, recorded, on the elder's terms.

**Owners for audit:** older adult (the whole kit, the twelve questions especially) · trans auditors (care-and-consent notes, discreet mode) · adolescent (reads for "does this ask anyone to disclose").

---

## Before you open this (read this first)

This kit doesn't ask anyone to come out, be outed, or explain themselves. Not the elder, not the person holding the phone, not the cousin at Umoja night.

Here's the whole point of this kit, said once: your elder came up under different rules. They may never have used the words you use. They may have had a "roommate" for thirty years, or a "friend" who came to every funeral. That's theirs. Nothing here asks them to translate it. The questions ask about life: houses, kitchens, churches, friends, the people who mattered. The elder says as much or as little as they choose. What they leave out is also the story.

It shipped to you in discreet mode. The email said "Your holidayz.vip kit." The file is named `holidayz-winter-kit-04.pdf`. The cover reads as a winter kit. If you're the elder, or the grandchild, and the house isn't safe for this yet, you can own it.

**What this asks of you**
- Time: interview 45–60 minutes, in one sitting or three; letter 30 minutes; inventory 20; Umoja night 45 minutes on Dec 26. About three hours across the season.
- Sensory: one voice at a time. A phone on the table, not in anyone's face. Candles on Umoja night (battery ones are fine). No required touch. Sitting the whole time is fine; so is lying down.
- Low-demand version: skip to "The bare minimum" at the end. One question, one recording, one candle. It counts.

## 1. The oral-history interview (twelve questions, one phone)

**Who holds the phone.** The elder, or the person the elder picks. The phone sits on the table between you, recording on the plain voice-memo app it came with. No app to download. Nothing uploads anywhere unless the elder sends it somewhere.

**Who owns the recording.** The elder. It stays on the phone the elder chose. When the interview's done, the elder decides who gets a copy, and when, and whether. "Nobody, not yet" is a full answer. So is "play it at my funeral." So is "delete it." If it's the younger person's phone, they send it to the elder and delete theirs, unless the elder says keep it.

**How to ask.** Read the question. Then be quiet, longer than feels normal. Elders don't need help filling silence; younger people need help not filling it. If the elder passes, say "okay" and read the next one. Don't circle back. The house rule is the same as every kit in this line: anyone can pass.

**The twelve.** Ask them in this order, or any order, or ask three and stop.

1. "Where's the first house you remember? Who was in it?"
2. "What did the kitchen smell like on a holiday when you were small? Who was cooking?"
3. "Who taught you to make the thing you're known for?"
4. "Who was your first real friend? Do you know where they are now?"
5. "Tell me about a church, or a place that was like church for you. One you stayed in, or one you left."
6. "Who was the person you could call at two in the morning? Who calls you?"
7. "Where did you live when you felt most like yourself?"
8. "Who did you love? Say a name if you want to. Or just tell me what they were like."
9. "What did people get wrong about you, back then? What do they get wrong now?"
10. "Was there a time you had to be careful? About anything. You don't have to say what."
11. "What's a song, a dish, a saying, or a habit you want still in this family in fifty years?"
12. "What do you want the youngest person in this family to know about you?"

**What the questions are doing.** Every one can be answered fully by an elder who never uses a single word the younger person would use. "Who did you love" has an answer that is a name and an answer that is "somebody good to me," and both are whole. Question 10 is about safety, not identity. The kit never asks "when did you know" or "did the family know." Those are the younger person's questions. This isn't their interview.

**Time:** 45–60 minutes, or 15 minutes a night for three nights. **Sensory:** one voice, one phone, a quiet room, TV off; if the elder is hard of hearing, sit on their good side and read slower, not louder. **Low-demand:** ask questions 1, 8, and 12. Twelve minutes. Stop.

## 2. The legacy letter (a template, not a form)

Written by the elder, alone, or with somebody they trust holding the pen. It's for one person or for everybody. It can be four lines. The template is there because a blank page is hard; nothing has to go in the blanks.

```
To ______________________,

What I want you to know about me:

What I want you to keep:

What I'm not going to explain, and don't need to:

What I hope for you:

______________________ (however you sign, or don't)
```

**The third line is the one that matters.** "What I'm not going to explain, and don't need to" is where the elder puts the whole of whatever they carried without the family ever needing the word for it. Some elders will write a name there. Some will write "you know." Some will leave it blank, and a blank there is a sentence.

**Who reads it.** The elder decides: sealed until a date, read at Umoja night, handed over and never mentioned, kept in a drawer. The kit has no step where the letter gets read.

**If the elder doesn't write.** They say it; somebody writes it down and reads it back until the elder says "that's right." The writer is a pen.

**Time:** 30 minutes, more if it's going well. **Sensory:** alone, or one other person; paper and a real pen if the elder likes paper, the phone's notes app if they don't. **Low-demand:** one line. "What I want you to keep:" and one thing after it.

## 3. "What I want you to keep" (the inventory)

This is not a will. Say that out loud before you start, so nobody's stomach drops. It's a list of things and who they go to, and, if the elder wants, why. Things means things: the cast-iron skillet, the church hat, the photo of the two people on the porch that nobody ever asked about, the name every dog in this family has had.

```
| The thing | Who it goes to | Why (optional; one word is plenty) |
|-----------|----------------|------------------------------------|
|           |                |                                    |
```

Ten rows. Print more if you need more. Recipes count. Sayings count. Photos count; the elder says who's in them, or doesn't.

**Who fills it in.** The elder says; somebody writes. The younger person doesn't suggest items. If they want something, they say so once, and the elder can say no.

**Where it lives.** With the elder, and one copy with the person they name. It doesn't come back to us, nobody signs it, and it isn't a will.

**Time:** 20 minutes. **Sensory:** from a chair, in a kitchen, with the game on low. It's the easiest piece in the kit; if the elder's tired, start here. **Low-demand:** three rows. Skillet, hat, photo. Done.

---

## 4. Umoja night (Dec 26, the first night)

This is the first night of Kwanzaa, and it's a real one, not a theme. Umoja means unity: the family that came is the family. The kinara goes on the table, the black candle in the middle gets lit first, and the elder lights it or picks who does. If the house doesn't keep Kwanzaa, keep this night anyway; one candle and one question is enough to start.

**The run (45 minutes, or 15).**

| When | What | Who |
|---|---|---|
| Before | Kinara or one candle set. Phone with the recording nearby, if the elder wants it there. A chair where the elder can see the room. | Whoever's hosting |
| Light | The black candle. Somebody says "Umoja. Unity." That's the whole opening. | The elder, or who they pick |
| Listen (optional) | The elder plays one piece of the recording, up to five minutes, or reads one line of the letter, or does neither. Nobody asks to hear more. | The elder |
| The question | Everybody, youngest to oldest: "Who in this family showed up for you this year?" One name each. Anyone can pass. | Everyone |
| Hands | Everyone writes one thing for the elder's "keep" list on a slip. Slips go to the elder, who reads them later, or never. | Everyone |
| Close | The elder says one thing, or "that's enough," or nothing. Candle stays lit through dinner. | The elder |

**Why this night and not the last one.** Unity is the elder's night because the elder is the reason most of the people in the room know each other. The night says that without a speech.

**Soul Compass (three lines, kept in the house).** After the candle, before dessert, one person reads these and everybody answers in a word or a pass. Somebody writes the words on the back of the Umoja card. The card stays in the house; nobody's word leaves it or gets explained.
- "Tonight I felt: ___."
- "One thing we did right: ___."
- "One thing for next year: ___."

That's the check-in. It isn't a score. It goes in the drawer with the letter.

**Time:** 45 minutes; 15 for the short version. **Sensory:** candlelight (battery is fine), one voice at a time, food after; if the recording plays, keep it short and at a volume the elder sets. **Low-demand:** light the candle, ask the one question, eat. Skip the slips.

## 5. Care and consent (for the younger people, mostly)

Read this before you open the phone. It's short because it's mostly one idea.

**Your elder came up under different rules.** Being seen cost jobs, church, family, housing, sometimes safety. Some of your elders paid it and some found another way to live a whole life. The second group is not less brave, and this kit isn't here to fix anything for them. They may never have used the words you use. They may not want to now. They may want to, at ninety, for the first time, into a phone in a kitchen. All three are theirs to decide.

**What that means in practice.**
- Don't introduce the interview with a label. "I want to record you" is enough. Not "I want to record your story as a ___."
- Don't ask the follow-up. If the elder says "my friend Ruth," Ruth is the answer. You don't need what you already know on tape.
- Don't post it. Not a clip, not a quote, not "so proud of my grandmother." The recording isn't yours.
- Ask before every step, not once at the start. "Can I record this part?" "Should the family hear that, or just me?" It's slower. Slower is the point.
- If your elder's memory comes and goes: ask on the good days, ask again on the next good day, and let the person the elder chose hold the recording. Yesterday's yes counts for yesterday.
- If your elder says something you didn't know, and it changes things for you: that's yours to carry, not theirs to manage. Say "thank you." Go for a walk. Don't make the next question about you.

**For the elder, if you're the one reading this.** You can say anything into this phone, or nothing. You can tell them to turn it off. You can tell a story you've told a hundred times and let that be the recording. You can use whatever word you've always used for whoever you loved, or none. The family gets to keep you, in your voice, exactly as far as you want to be kept. That's the whole thing.

**Time:** 5 minutes to read. **Sensory:** none. **Low-demand:** the first bold line and the third bullet.

## 6. The bare minimum (low-demand version)

If the elder is tired, or the family's only together for one afternoon, or it's just the two of you:

1. Put the phone on the table. Ask question 8: "Who did you love?" Let them answer or pass. Stop recording.
2. Fill one line of the inventory. Skillet, hat, photo, anything.
3. On Dec 26, light one candle. Say "Umoja." Eat.

That's a legacy. The elder's voice is on a phone the elder owns. Everything else is extra.

## Open questions for the auditors (round 1)

- Older adult: the twelve questions. Which one would you refuse? Which would you answer and then wish you hadn't? Is question 10 ("was there a time you had to be careful") a door or a trap?
- Older adult: "What I'm not going to explain, and don't need to." Is that line a gift, or does it point too hard? Keep it or cut it?
- Older adult: Umoja night puts you at the front of the room with a candle. Honor or spotlight? What would make it the first and not the second?
- Trans auditors: "they may never have used the words you use." Does that respect the elder, or does it quietly assume the younger person's words are the right ones? What's the sentence you'd write instead?
- Trans auditors: does the recording mechanic (elder's phone, elder sends copies) fail in a real house where the elder's phone is set up and managed by an adult child?
- Adolescent: if this were your grandmother, is there a single line here that would make you close the PDF?
- Everyone: after page one, the kit never uses "queer" or any identity word. Right for this kit, or does it make the elder invisible?
