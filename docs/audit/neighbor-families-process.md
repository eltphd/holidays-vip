# Auditing the kits with neighbor families

A kitchen-table version of the community audit. Same consent, same scorecard, same rules. Different recruiting: the people already within walking distance, read at a real table, on paper, in one evening. Built for the compressed timeline: recruit this week, read Oct 12, close Oct 18.

## Why neighbors

- They are the buyer. A family with a teen, an elder, a kitchen. No lens table needed to find them.
- They say no faster and yes faster. A text, not an email chain.
- Kids are in the room, which is the only way to test "would this embarrass me."
- A kit read at a table by people who didn't write it is the audit. The rubric just writes it down.

## Who you need (four to six households)

| Seat | Who on your block | Reads |
|---|---|---|
| Elder | a grandmother, a retired neighbor, anyone over 65 who cooks | Nourish |
| Teen | a 14–18 year old, parent present | Teen Room (and skims one other for "would this embarrass me") |
| Parents of teens | the household next door | Family Weather |
| Host type | the one who throws the block party | Open House |
| Adult child | someone who goes "home" for the holidays | Coming Home |

**Whole Table needs the right readers.** Any neighbor can score line 7, outing risk ("would a hostile relative learn anything from this?"). Lines 2 and 4, representation and peer register, need LGBTQIA+ readers. If your block doesn't give you one, bring one person from your own network for Open House and Coming Home, or those two kits ship on the design partners' word only and the site says so. Don't ask any neighbor whether they qualify. Offer every kit to every household; let people pick.

## The ask (60 seconds, at the door or by text)

> I make holiday kits for families like ours. Five of them are drafts and I need real people to read one at their kitchen table and tell me what's wrong before December. One evening, dinner's on me, you keep a kit. Read it as yourself. If it's not how we talk, say so. Anyone can pass.

Text version:

> Hey, it's Erica from [street]. I'm testing holiday kits for Black families and I'd rather you tell me it's wrong than a stranger in December. One evening at my place Sunday Oct 12, 6–8, dinner included, you keep a kit. Teens welcome, with you. Yes or no is fine, no explanation needed.

## Two formats, pick per household

**Kit Night (preferred).** One evening, your table or a neighbor's, 4–6 households, 90 minutes plus food.

| Time | What |
|---|---|
| 0:00 | Food first. Consent page on the table; walk it through out loud (3 minutes); people sign or say "I agree" and you note it. |
| 0:20 | Hand each household its printed kit. 30 minutes, read silently, pen in hand. Three things to mark: what would make you put it down, what asks somebody to explain themselves, what's just not how we talk. |
| 0:50 | Go around. One household at a time, five minutes each: the one thing you'd cut, the one thing you'd keep. You write; nobody records. |
| 1:20 | Scorecard, eight lines, 0–2. Fill it alone, hand it in. |
| 1:30 | Done. Everyone leaves with the kit they read and a code for a second one in December. |

**Take-home.** For the household that can't come: drop the printed kit, the consent page, and the scorecard in an envelope. Pick it up in five days. Ten minutes at their door when you collect: the same two questions.

## Consent, kitchen-table version

Same one page (`consent-form.md`). What changes is how it's handled:

- Read it out loud, don't hand it over silently. Three minutes.
- Signature or a typed "I agree" by text, either counts. Date it.
- Teens: parent at the table, parent signs too. No teen reads alone with you.
- Credit choice and pay choice get asked once, written on the back, done.
- Contact details stay in your sheet. The database gets a first name and a seat.

## What you capture (one sheet per household)

```
Household handle: ______   Seat: ______   Kit: ______   Date: ______
The one thing they'd put it down for:
The one thing that asks someone to explain themselves:
The one thing that's not how we talk:
The one thing they'd keep exactly:
Scorecard attached: Y/N   Any zero: Y/N   on line: __
Pay: kit / $75 / neither   Credit: named / first name / none
```

Any zero stops that kit. Say so to the person on the spot: "that's a zero, so it's stopped until it's fixed." That sentence is why they'll read carefully.

## After the evening

1. Photo the capture sheets and scorecards to yourself. Keep the paper.
2. Fill `auditors-template.csv` (handles and seats only). Send it to Claude; it loads in a minute.
3. Send Claude the capture sheets. Reviews get entered against each kit, zeros flagged, and round-2 edits start the next day.
4. Round 2 is the same evening again on Oct 26 or 27, if the same households will come, or take-home for everyone. Shorter: 20 minutes, "here's what changed because of you."

## Compensation that works on a block

- Dinner on the night, and the kit they read, printed, is theirs.
- A code for one Complete bundle of their choice when it ships, or $75. Their pick.
- Teens: the kit to the household, never cash to the teen.
- Eight households at $75 is $600, the budget line. Kits are cheaper and most people pick the kit.

## What this doesn't change

The rubric is the rubric. A zero is a zero. Nobody is asked to disclose anything. Nothing is recorded. Names on the credits page are opt-in only. The warmline card and the discreet-mode rules are not up for a vote; everything else is.
